Three worlds, one starting point

Same seed, same hundred entities with the same skills and temperaments. The private and co-op worlds differ in how firms are organised; the private and central-bank worlds differ only in how money works. Money is counted in X in the first two and in crowns in the third, so these measures avoid comparing the two units directly.

How this world works

Money by fiat. Nobody has a budget in their own currency. Paying a wage or buying something creates new units of the payer's currency. When a business is paid in its owner's currency, those units are retired.

The exchange. Each currency has a pool that trades it against X. X has a fixed supply. To buy from someone, a shopper sells their own money for X and buys the seller's money with it, so printing pushes a currency down and customers pull it up.

Work and business, private world. Everyone has different skill at making food, goods and services. Businesses set prices and wages in their owner's currency, hire when they keep selling out and lay off when stock piles up. Workers take the job whose pay converts to the most X, so a sliding currency loses its staff.

Work and co-ops, cooperative world. Nobody employs anybody. A co-op sets its price in X, and every sale is split equally among its members, each paid in their own currency. There are no wages. Members vote a newcomer in when the co-op keeps selling out and the newcomer would not cut their share by much, and turn away applicants with much less skill than the members have. When stock piles up, the members vote out whoever joined last; the founder always stays. Idle entities team up in twos and threes to found new co-ops.

Bonds. An issuer sells bonds for its own currency, which takes that money out of circulation today. At maturity, 30 ticks later, it prints the principal plus interest. Buyers demand a yield that beats the currency's recent slide; issuers stop selling above 60% per term.

Central-bank world. The same firms, wages and labour market as the private world, but nobody issues money. There is one currency, the crown. Crowns are created when the banking system lends and destroyed when loans are repaid. One central bank sets the policy rate with a Taylor rule: up when inflation runs above its target, down when unemployment rises. Loans cost the rate plus a spread, and the interest goes to depositors. Entities borrow more when the real loan rate is low and when they run short, up to a limit set by their income. Twenty ticks behind on payments means default: the loan is written off and credit is frozen for 60 ticks. Here the printing-appetite slider sets the inflation target.

Policy. Each central bank sits somewhere between hawk and dove. Doves print more and tolerate a faster slide. When a currency falls faster than its issuer tolerates, the issuer stops printing, sells more bonds and spends X reserves to buy its money back. When it rises too fast, the issuer prints more.

Spending grows with savings. In every world, an entity holding well over the reserve it aims for wants more goods and services as its surplus grows, up to five times its usual wants. Without this, savings nobody spends drain demand from a world with one fixed money.

How they feel. Each entity has six feelings: being fed, comfort, security (X reserves and having work), purpose (owning a firm beats a job, which beats idleness; co-op members count as owners), pride in its currency (in the central-bank world, confidence about its finances: steady prices and manageable debt), and standing next to everyone else. Mood is that level plus how it compares with what the entity has grown used to, so a fall hurts more than steady hardship and good times fade into normal. Diaries record what happens to them in their own words.

Save files. Save run writes either a full save, which holds the whole history and the exact state so the run can be loaded and continued, or a short summary for pasting into a chat. Every settings change is logged with the tick it happened.

Redenomination. A currency that falls below a millionth of an X is replaced at 1,000,000 old for 1 new. Its value history carries on unbroken.
